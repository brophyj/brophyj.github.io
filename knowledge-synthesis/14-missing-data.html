Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • distinguish the kinds of data that can be missing in a meta-analysis, and the tools for each;
  • define MCAR, MAR and MNAR, recognise which is plausible in a given trial, and explain why MAR cannot be verified;
  • explain, with a simulation, what complete-case analysis, LOCF, BOCF, single and multiple imputation and mixed models each assume, and when each is biased;
  • recover missing standard deviations from confidence intervals, P values, medians and quartiles, and explain why change-score SDs depend on an unknown correlation;
  • apply the informative missingness odds ratio (IMOR) to a meta-analysis of binary outcomes, including different assumptions by arm, a tipping-point analysis and an uncertain IMOR;
  • extract and report missing-data information, and carry it into risk of bias and GRADE.

R packages: dplyr, tidyr, purrr, ggplot2, metafor, mice, nlme, knitr; metamedian and metasens for code that runs where they are installed.

Every meta-analysis has missing data. Whole studies may be unpublished, outcomes may be measured but not reported, summary statistics may be incomplete, and within each trial some participants’ outcomes are never observed. This chapter deals mainly with the last two: missing participants and missing summary statistics. Missing studies, the subject of publication bias, are taken up in Chapter 15.

14.1 The scale of the problem

In a review of 77 trials published in the BMJ, JAMA, Lancet and NEJM in the second half of 2013, 95% reported some missing outcome data, with a median of 9% of participants missing (range 0% to 70%). The primary analysis used complete cases in 45%, simple imputation such as last observation carried forward in 27%, model-based methods in 19% and multiple imputation in 8%; only 35% reported a sensitivity analysis (1). In a review of 235 trials with a statistically significant binary outcome in five leading journals (median loss to follow-up 6%), 0% to 33% of results were no longer significant under plausible assumptions about the participants lost (2).

For the meta-analyst the problem compounds. A trial reports a number analysed, rarely the mechanism of missingness, and sometimes not even the method used to handle it. Different trials use different methods, and pooling them combines estimates that may answer slightly different questions, weighted by size rather than by data quality.

Missing study-level data mainly limit which questions can be asked; a subgroup analysis restricted to the trials that chose to report the subgroup is itself open to selective reporting. Missing participants bias the answers.

14.2 Mechanisms of missingness

Three mechanisms, defined by Rubin, describe how the probability that an outcome is missing relates to the data.

  • Missing completely at random (MCAR). Missingness depends on neither observed nor unobserved data: \(P(\text{missing} \mid Y_{obs}, Y_{mis}) = P(\text{missing})\). The complete cases are a random subsample, and analysing them is unbiased, with some loss of precision. A dropped laboratory sample is the classic example; in trials, MCAR is rare.
  • Missing at random (MAR). Missingness depends only on observed data: \(P(\text{missing} \mid Y_{obs}, Y_{mis}) = P(\text{missing} \mid Y_{obs})\). Older or sicker patients may drop out more, but given their age and baseline severity, dropout is random. Analyses that condition on the variables that predict missingness (multiple imputation, mixed models) are valid.
  • Missing not at random (MNAR). Missingness depends on the unobserved outcome itself, even after conditioning on everything observed. Patients who are not improving stop attending; patients with adverse effects withdraw. No analysis of the observed data alone is guaranteed valid.

The distinction between MAR and MNAR cannot be tested from the data, because it concerns values that were never observed. Figure 14.1 simulates the same 2000 patients under each mechanism.

Under MCAR the observed mean is unbiased. Under MAR the patients who were sicker at baseline drop out, so the observed mean looks too good; because baseline is observed, adjusting for it removes the bias. Under MNAR, dropout depends on the outcome itself, and adjustment for baseline helps only partly.

In most drug trials MNAR is plausible: patients who are not improving stop treatment, patients with adverse effects withdraw, and deteriorating patients become too ill to attend. Yet trials’ primary analyses almost always assume MCAR or MAR. The recommended practice is a primary analysis under a plausible MAR assumption plus sensitivity analyses that explore departures towards MNAR (3). If dropout differs between arms for reasons related to the outcome, the comparison of completers is no longer randomised, and the bias can favour either arm. The STRIDE trial assessed in Chapter 5 is an example: about 15% of participants were missing at 52 weeks, more in the placebo arm, and placebo patients who were not improving plausibly left more often.

No analysis can recover information that was never collected. Trial features that reduce missing data (follow-up continued after treatment is stopped, flexible dosing, rescue medication built into the protocol, outcomes less prone to missingness) point to a lower risk of bias in RoB 2 domain 3. Some designs reduce missing data at a price: run-in periods and randomised withdrawal designs exclude, before randomisation, patients who cannot tolerate or adhere to the drug, so that harms are underestimated and the results apply to a selected group, an issue of indirectness rather than risk of bias.

14.3 Handling methods and their assumptions

Every method for handling missing outcomes is an assumption about what those outcomes would have been. Knowing which assumption each trial made tells the meta-analyst which trials may be biased, and in which direction.

14.3.1 One simulated trial, three mechanisms

To see these assumptions at work, we simulate a trial of a depression-type score (higher is worse), measured at baseline and three follow-up visits in 150 patients per arm. Both arms improve; by visit 3 the active arm has improved 6 points more (true effect −6). Patients drop out with the same rule in both arms: under MCAR, 10% of those remaining at each visit by chance; under MAR, with a probability that rises with lack of improvement at the previous (observed) visit; under MNAR, with lack of improvement at the current (unobserved) visit. Under MAR and MNAR the placebo arm, with more non-improvers, loses more patients. Each scenario is simulated 150 times, and each simulated trial is analysed six ways, all adjusting for baseline.

Code 
sim_trial <- function(n = 150, mech = "MCAR", effect = -6) {
  trt <- rep(0:1, each = n)
  u   <- rnorm(2 * n, 0, 4)                                   # patient level
  s   <- rnorm(2 * n, 0, 6)                                   # patient response
  Y   <- sapply(0:3, \(t) 30 + u + (-3 + trt * effect / 3 + s / 3) * t + rnorm(2 * n, 0, 4))
  R   <- matrix(TRUE, 2 * n, 4)                               # TRUE = observed
  for (t in 2:4) {
    worse_prev <- Y[, t - 1] - Y[, 1] + 3 * (t - 2)           # lack of improvement, previous visit
    worse_now  <- Y[, t]     - Y[, 1] + 3 * (t - 1)           # ... current visit (unseen if missing)
    lp <- switch(mech, MCAR = rep(-2.2, 2 * n),               # plogis(-2.2) = 10% per visit
                 MAR = -2.2 + 0.3 * worse_prev, MNAR = -2.2 + 0.3 * worse_now)
    R[, t] <- R[, t - 1] & rbinom(2 * n, 1, plogis(lp)) == 0  # monotone dropout
  }
  Yobs <- Y; Yobs[!R] <- NA
  list(Y = Y, Yobs = Yobs, trt = trt)
}
coefse <- function(fit, term = "trt") {
  s <- if (inherits(fit, "gls")) summary(fit)$tTable else summary(fit)$coefficients
  c(est = s[term, 1], se = s[term, 2])
}
analyse <- function(d) {
  Yo <- d$Yobs; trt <- d$trt; y0 <- Yo[, 1]; y3 <- Yo[, 4]
  last <- apply(Yo, 1, \(r) tail(na.omit(r), 1))
  out <- list(
    `No missing data` = coefse(lm(d$Y[, 4] ~ trt + y0)),     # benchmark, before dropout
    `Complete case`   = coefse(lm(y3 ~ trt + y0)),
    LOCF              = coefse(lm(last ~ trt + y0)),
    BOCF              = coefse(lm(ifelse(is.na(y3), y0, y3) ~ trt + y0)),
    `Mean imputation` = coefse(lm(ifelse(is.na(y3), ave(y3, trt, FUN = \(x) mean(x, na.rm = TRUE)), y3) ~ trt + y0)))
  imp <- mice(data.frame(trt, y0, y1 = Yo[, 2], y2 = Yo[, 3], y3), m = 5, method = "norm", printFlag = FALSE)
  pm  <- summary(pool(with(imp, lm(y3 ~ trt + y0))))
  out$`Multiple imputation` <- c(est = pm$estimate[2], se = pm$std.error[2])
  N    <- length(trt)
  long <- tibble::tibble(id = rep(1:N, 3), trt = rep(trt, 3), y0 = rep(y0, 3),
                         visit = factor(rep(1:3, each = N)), y = c(Yo[, 2:4])) |> filter(!is.na(y))
  mm <- gls(y ~ visit * trt + visit * y0, data = long,           # mixed model for repeated measures
            correlation = corSymm(form = ~ as.integer(visit) | id),
            weights = varIdent(form = ~ 1 | visit))
  V  <- vcov(mm)[c("trt", "visit3:trt"), c("trt", "visit3:trt")]
  out$`Mixed model (MMRM)` <- c(est = sum(coef(mm)[c("trt", "visit3:trt")]), se = sqrt(sum(V)))
  bind_rows(out, .id = "method")
}
set.seed(629)
sims <- map_dfr(c("MCAR", "MAR", "MNAR"), \(m) map_dfr(1:150, \(r) {
  d <- sim_trial(mech = m)
  analyse(d) |> mutate(mech = m, miss_pbo = mean(is.na(d$Yobs[d$trt == 0, 4])),
                       miss_act = mean(is.na(d$Yobs[d$trt == 1, 4])))
}))
sim_summary <- sims |>
  mutate(mech = factor(mech, c("MCAR", "MAR", "MNAR")),
         method = factor(method, c("No missing data", "Complete case", "LOCF", "BOCF",
                                   "Mean imputation", "Multiple imputation", "Mixed model (MMRM)"))) |>
  group_by(mech, method) |>
  summarise(est_mean = mean(est), lo = mean(est - 1.96 * se), hi = mean(est + 1.96 * se),
            coverage = mean(abs(est + 6) < 1.96 * se),
            miss_pbo = mean(miss_pbo), miss_act = mean(miss_act), .groups = "drop")
miss_rates <- sim_summary |> distinct(mech, miss_pbo, miss_act)
Code 
sim_summary |>
  mutate(method = forcats::fct_rev(method), cov_lab = sprintf("%.0f%%", 100 * coverage)) |>
  ggplot(aes(est_mean, method, colour = mech)) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = -6, linetype = "dashed", colour = "grey30") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 0, colour = "grey60") +

  geom_text(aes(x = 1.8, label = cov_lab, group = forcats::fct_rev(mech)), size = 3.3,

  coord_cartesian(xlim = c(-9, 2.3)) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c(MCAR = "#2E7D32", MAR = "#E69F00", MNAR = "#B22222"), name = NULL) +
  labs(x = "Estimated treatment effect (true = −6)", y = NULL) +
  theme(legend.position = "top")

At visit 3 the placebo arm lost about 43% of patients under MNAR, the active arm about 26%. Reading Figure 14.2 method by method:

  • Complete case is unbiased under MCAR, at the cost of precision. Under MAR and MNAR the placebo arm loses more of its non-improvers, so its completers look better than the arm as a whole, and the benefit is understated. Complete-case analysis assumes that the missing patients in each arm would have done, on average, like the observed patients in that arm.
  • LOCF and BOCF are biased towards the null even under MCAR, not because patients improve, but because the gap between the arms widens over time and the dropouts are frozen at an earlier visit, or at baseline, when the arms had separated less. Under MAR and MNAR they land nearer the truth than complete case, because their pull towards the null happens to offset the placebo arm’s selective losses: luck, not validity. A “conservative” method is conservative only for one direction of one comparison; in a non-inferiority trial, bias towards the null favours the new drug.
  • Mean imputation has about the same bias as complete case, but treats imputed values as data, so its intervals are too narrow.
  • Multiple imputation and the mixed model (MMRM) are close to unbiased under MCAR and MAR, and give nearly the same answers. Under MNAR both are biased in the same way: the information that would correct them was never measured.

Figure 14.3 illustrates why LOCF misleads for a condition that changes over time.

For the meta-analyst the practical lessons are to prefer a trial’s multiple-imputation or mixed-model estimate to its LOCF estimate when both are reported; to avoid mixing LOCF, BOCF and MMRM estimates without at least a sensitivity analysis by method; and to remember that neither MI nor MMRM removes the need to examine departures from MAR.

14.3.2 Worst and best cases

For a binary outcome the missing participants can be assigned the most extreme outcomes. Consider one trial in which the outcome is an adverse event (RR < 1 favours treatment): 12 events among 55 analysed of 63 randomised to treatment, and 22 among 55 analysed of 60 controls. Because this is an illustration, we also know that 4 of the 8 treatment dropouts and 2 of the 5 control dropouts had the event.

Code 
rr <- function(et, nt, ec, nc) {
  e <- escalc("RR", ai = et, n1i = nt, ci = ec, n2i = nc)
  x <- exp(e$yi + c(0, -1.96, 1.96) * sqrt(e$vi))
  sprintf("%.2f (%.2f to %.2f)", x[1], x[2], x[3])
}
tibble::tibble(
  Scenario = c("Truth: everyone counted", "Complete case", "Missing = no event",
               "Worst case for treatment", "Best case for treatment"),
  `RR (95% CI)` = c(rr(12 + 4, 63, 22 + 2, 60), rr(12, 55, 22, 55), rr(12, 63, 22, 60),
                    rr(12 + 8, 63, 22, 60), rr(12, 63, 22 + 5, 60))) |>
  kable(align = "lc")
Scenario RR (95% CI)
Truth: everyone counted 0.63 (0.38 to 1.07)
Complete case 0.55 (0.30 to 0.99)
Missing = no event 0.52 (0.28 to 0.95)
Worst case for treatment 0.87 (0.53 to 1.42)
Best case for treatment 0.42 (0.24 to 0.76)

The truth lies between the bounds, as it must. Complete case overstates the benefit, because the treatment dropouts had the event more often (4 of 8) than the treatment completers (12 of 55). Bounds are usually too wide to be informative, which is why GRADE recommends plausible worst cases rather than extreme ones (Section 14.6).

14.4 Missing summary statistics

Continuous outcomes need a mean and an SD (or SE) for each arm, but papers often report only means with P values, confidence intervals, or medians and ranges. This is missing summary information, not missing participants, and much of it can be recovered (4).

  • From a 95% confidence interval for a mean: \(SE = (\text{upper} - \text{lower})/(2 \times 1.96)\), using \(t_{n-1}\) in place of 1.96 for small samples, and \(SD = SE\sqrt{n}\).
  • From a t statistic or exact P value for a difference: \(SE_{\text{diff}} = |\bar X_1 - \bar X_2|/t\), and \(SD = SE_{\text{diff}}/\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}\).
  • When nothing can be calculated: ask the authors; otherwise borrow an SD from other studies in the review, which performs reasonably well empirically (5). For a mean difference a larger imputed SD only down-weights the study; for a standardised mean difference it shrinks the effect towards zero. If most studies lack SDs, do not impute; and always show the effect of removing the imputed studies.
Code 
md <- 5.0; p <- 0.03; n1 <- n2 <- 40                    # "MD 5.0 points, p = 0.03, 40 per arm"
t_val <- qt(1 - p / 2, df = n1 + n2 - 2)
se_md <- md / t_val
round(c(t = t_val, SE = se_md, SD = se_md / sqrt(1 / n1 + 1 / n2)), 2)
    t    SE    SD 
 2.21  2.26 10.12

Only exact P values can be inverted; “P < 0.05” cannot. P values are rounded (0.03 could be 0.025 to 0.035), so check the sensitivity of the recovered SD. If the trial used ANCOVA or a mixed model, the recovered SD is a residual SD, smaller than the raw SD, and should not be mixed with raw SDs in a standardised mean difference.

14.4.1 Change scores and the unknown correlation

To pool change from baseline, each arm needs the SD of the change, which many trials do not report:

\[SD_{\text{change}} = \sqrt{SD_{\text{base}}^2 + SD_{\text{final}}^2 - 2\,r\,SD_{\text{base}}\,SD_{\text{final}}},\]

where r is the correlation between baseline and final values, almost never reported. It can be estimated from studies that report all three SDs and borrowed for those that do not, but it is an assumption about missing data and should be varied. Consider a small trial of fluoxetine and nortriptyline in children with depression (20 per arm), reporting the Children’s Depression Inventory as 28.9 (SD 8.4) → 17.9 (8.1) with fluoxetine and 28.4 (8.7) → 25.8 (8.6) with nortriptyline.

Code 
sd_change <- \(sb, sf, r) sqrt(sb^2 + sf^2 - 2 * r * sb * sf)
diff_change <- (28.9 - 17.9) - (28.4 - 25.8)
tibble::tibble(r = c(0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 0.97)) |>
  mutate(`SD change, fluoxetine` = sd_change(8.4, 8.1, r),
         `SD change, nortriptyline` = sd_change(8.7, 8.6, r),
         `Pooled SD` = sqrt((`SD change, fluoxetine`^2 + `SD change, nortriptyline`^2) / 2),
         `Change-score SMD` = diff_change / `Pooled SD`) |>
  kable(digits = 2, align = "ccccc")

With a typical r of 0.3 to 0.7 the SMD is about 0.8 to 1.3; at r = 0.97 it is about 4. When the trial’s ANCOVA estimate is available, use it. For a mean difference, change scores and final values can be pooled together and final values need no r; for an SMD they should not be mixed, because change-score SDs are smaller and inflate the SMD.

14.4.2 Medians, quartiles and ranges

If the outcome is roughly normal, quartiles and extremes carry information about the mean and SD. In a normal distribution the interquartile range spans 1.35 SD, so \(SD \approx IQR/1.35\) in large samples. The range depends on sample size: in a sample of 10 it spans about 3 SD, in a sample of 1000 about 6.5. (6) proposed the first formulas; (7) replaced fixed divisors with ones that change with n; (8) improved the estimate of the mean.

Code 
eta    <- \(n) 2 * qnorm((0.75 * n - 0.125) / (n + 0.25))     # expected IQR of n standard normals
wan_s2 <- \(q1, m, q3, n) c(mean = (q1 + m + q3) / 3, sd = (q3 - q1) / eta(n))
tibble::tribble(~study, ~n, ~q1, ~med, ~q3,
                "Study 1", 45, 285, 320, 360,
                "Study 2", 112, 260, 298, 345,
                "Study 3", 28, 300, 335, 375) |>
  mutate(`η(n)` = eta(n), est = pmap(list(q1, med, q3, n), wan_s2),
         `Estimated mean` = map_dbl(est, "mean"), `Estimated SD` = map_dbl(est, "sd")) |>
  select(-est) |>
  kable(digits = 2)

The Cochrane Handbook adds cautions (4). A paper that reports a median and IQR is often signalling a skewed outcome (length of stay, costs, biomarkers), for which these formulas may mislead; ranges are unstable and should generally not be used to estimate SDs. Check skewness, for example with Bowley’s coefficient \((Q_1 + Q_3 - 2\,\text{median})/IQR\), use the conversions for a minority of studies, and show the effect of omitting them. The metamedian package implements these and more flexible quantile-based estimators and pools the result with metafor (9):

  `Hozo / Wan / Bland`  = metamean(dat.age, mean_method = "hozo/wan/bland"),

In this example, 29 of the 51 studies reported only medians and IQRs. Converting them lets all 51 be pooled; whether leaving them out matters cannot be known until they are converted.

14.5 Binary outcomes: the informative missingness odds ratio

For binary outcomes, a sensitivity analysis that works with aggregate data is the informative missingness odds ratio (IMOR) (10,11). Within one arm, it compares the odds of the outcome among the missing with the odds among the observed:

\[IMOR = \frac{\text{odds of success among the missing}}{\text{odds of success among the observed}} .\]

IMOR = 1 is complete-case analysis; IMOR < 1 means the missing did worse; IMOR = 0 means every missing participant failed; and IMOR → ∞ means every missing participant succeeded. IMOR cannot be estimated from the data; it is a sensitivity parameter chosen by clinical judgement. For example, if an arm has 40 improved, 20 not improved and 40 missing (odds 2), and a clinician thinks that 10 of the 40 missing would have improved (odds 1/3), the IMOR is 1/6.

14.5.2 Applying the IMOR

For each arm of each study: compute the observed risk p among the n analysed and the proportion missing π; convert the chosen IMOR into a risk among the missing, \(p_m = IMOR \cdot p/(IMOR \cdot p + 1 - p)\); and compute the risk for everyone randomised, \(p^* = (1-\pi)p + \pi p_m\). The variance of \(p^*\), by the delta method, has terms for sampling error in p, sampling error in π (zero when IMOR = 1), and, if the IMOR is uncertain, uncertainty in the IMOR itself. The adjusted log RRs are then pooled as usual. Adding guessed events to the 2×2 table and analysing them as if observed is wrong: trials with many missing participants would then look more precise, when they are less certain.

Code 
adj_arm <- function(r, f, m, imor, sd_log = 0, cc = 0) {    # one arm (Higgins, White & Wood 2008)
  N  <- r + f + m; pi <- m / N                               # randomised; proportion missing
  n  <- r + f + 2 * cc; p <- (r + cc) / n                    # analysed; observed risk
  pm <- imor * p / (imor * p + 1 - p)                        # risk among the missing
  ps <- (1 - pi) * p + pi * pm                               # risk among everyone randomised
  dp <- (1 - pi) + pi * imor / (imor * p + 1 - p)^2          # d ps / d p
  v  <- dp^2 * p * (1 - p) / n +                             # 1. sampling error in p
        (pm - p)^2 * pi * (1 - pi) / N +                     # 2. sampling error in pi
        (pi * pm * (1 - pm))^2 * sd_log^2                    # 3. uncertainty in log IMOR
  tibble::tibble(ps, v)
}
imor_ma <- function(d, imorH, imorP = imorH, sd_log = 0) {   # pooled log RR, common effect
  cc <- ifelse(with(d, rH * fH * rP * fP) == 0, 0.5, 0)
  h  <- with(d, adj_arm(rH, fH, mH, imorH, sd_log, cc))
  pl <- with(d, adj_arm(rP, fP, mP, imorP, sd_log, cc))
  rma(yi = log(h$ps / pl$ps), vi = h$v / h$ps^2 + pl$v / pl$ps^2, method = "EE")
}

14.5.3 Tipping points and uncertain IMORs

Patients may leave the two arms for different reasons, so the IMOR can differ by arm. Figure 14.4 maps the pooled RR over a grid of IMORs for each arm and marks where the 95% CI first includes 1: a tipping-point analysis.

Significance is lost only in the top-left corner, where missing haloperidol patients did much worse and missing placebo patients much better than the observed. Whether that is plausible is a clinical question, which is the point: the analysis converts a vague worry about missing data into a specific assumption that can be judged.

Rather than a single IMOR, our belief can be described by a distribution. Suppose, for these trials, a log IMOR centred on log(0.7) with SD 0.35: missing patients probably did somewhat worse than those observed, but not all failed. The uncertainty can enter analytically, through the variance term above, or by simulation, drawing an IMOR for each arm, re-running the meta-analysis, and pooling the results.

Code 
set.seed(629)
draws <- map_dbl(1:2000, \(i) {
  f <- imor_ma(haloperidol, exp(rnorm(1, -0.35, 0.35)), exp(rnorm(1, -0.35, 0.35)))
  rnorm(1, f$b[1], f$se)
})
an <- imor_ma(haloperidol, exp(-0.35), sd_log = 0.35)

Uncertainty about the missing data widens the interval; complete-case analysis pretends that uncertainty is zero.

14.6 What reviewers should do

The Cochrane Handbook recommends contacting investigators for missing data, stating the assumptions behind whatever method is used, assessing risk of bias due to missing outcome data, running sensitivity analyses over reasonable changes in those assumptions, and discussing the potential impact of missing data on the findings (12). Single imputation treated as observed data is not recommended; complete-case analysis is acceptable as a starting point; methods that allow for the uncertainty (multiple imputation, IMOR with its variance) are preferred.

For each arm of each study, extract the number randomised, the number analysed and at what time, the number missing and the reasons, the method used to handle missing data, and whether the primary analysis was intention-to-treat. The CONSORT flow diagram is the primary source, and RoB 2 domain 3 is judged for each outcome.

GRADE guidance 17 (13) starts from the complete-case meta-analysis and re-analyses under plausible worst-case assumptions: for binary outcomes, that participants with missing data in the intervention arms had higher event rates than those followed, by factors such as 1.5 to 5; for continuous outcomes, imputed means taken from other studies in the review. If the conclusion is robust to the most extreme plausible assumptions, do not rate down for risk of bias; if it is not, rate down. Applied to 100 meta-analyses with a significant result, plausible assumptions of this kind moved 6% to 22% across the null (14).

There is no sensible threshold for an acceptable proportion missing: the impact depends on the event rate and on how different the missing are, not only on how many there are. If 900 of 1000 patients are followed with 2% mortality and the 100 missing had 20% mortality, the true mortality is 3.8%, nearly double what was observed.

Key points

  1. Missing data are the rule. Distinguish missing studies, outcomes, summary statistics, participants and study-level data; each has its own tools.
  2. MCAR, MAR and MNAR describe how missingness relates to the data; MAR cannot be verified, and MNAR is often plausible in clinical trials.
  3. Complete-case analysis assumes the missing resemble the observed in each arm; LOCF and BOCF make implausible assumptions and can bias in either direction; multiple imputation and mixed models are valid under MAR but not MNAR.
  4. Missing SDs can often be recovered from intervals, exact P values or quartiles; change-score SDs depend on an unknown correlation, which should be varied.
  5. The IMOR framework turns assumptions about missing participants into explicit sensitivity analyses, including different assumptions by arm, tipping points and uncertain IMORs.
  6. Extract numbers randomised, analysed and missing, with reasons, per arm; judge RoB 2 domain 3; and follow GRADE guidance 17.
